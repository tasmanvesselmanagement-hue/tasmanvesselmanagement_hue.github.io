Same-day method

How to make it

1

Build the wet semi-biga

Mix 200 g flour, 5 g vital wheat gluten, 165 g warm water and 1.5 g active dry yeast until no dry flour remains. It should be loose, sticky and shaggy rather than a traditional stiff biga. Cover and leave warm until clearly expanded, bubbly and aromatic. Do not stare at it continuously; yeast performs poorly under surveillance.

About 2½–3 hours
2

Mix the final dough

Add 300 g flour, 5 g vital wheat gluten, 255 g water, 3 g active dry yeast, 10 g salt and 15 g olive oil. Mix thoroughly until homogeneous. Do not try to knead it like pizza dough — at this hydration it should be very wet. If it looks alarmingly loose, this is not a defect. This is character development.

5–8 minutes mixing
3

Build strength, gently

Use wet hands. Give the dough two stretch-and-folds while it is still loose, then one or two gentle coil folds once it begins holding together. Before each coil fold, ask the dough for consent. Silence may be interpreted as reluctant acceptance, but proceed respectfully. Roughly 20–30 minutes between folds is ideal, though longer gaps are absolutely fine. Stop once the dough feels elastic, glossy and emotionally secure.

3–4 consensual folds total
4

Bulk ferment

Cover and leave the dough alone until it looks aerated and lively. You want visible bubbles and some strength, not necessarily a precise doubling. High-hydration dough can move quickly once fermentation gets going. This is private time between the gluten and the yeast. Respect their boundaries.

Until airy and active
5

Oil a metal tray generously

Use a metal tray rather than ceramic for the crispest base. Coat it with 25–30 g olive oil. For full authenticity, verify that the olive was facing Mecca at the precise moment it was squeezed. Tip in the dough gently, turn it once so both sides are oily, and ease it toward the corners. If it springs back, leave it 15 minutes; negotiation is more effective than force.

Metal beats ceramic here
6

Final proof

Cover and leave in the tray until noticeably puffy, bubbly and jiggly. Don’t sacrifice the final proof just to squeeze in more folds — at this point you are protecting the gas you worked for. A gentle wobble is desirable. A violent slosh suggests you have created soup.

About 45–60 minutes
7

Dimple like you mean it

Drizzle 15–20 g olive oil over the top. Oil or wet your fingers and push firmly right down toward the tray to make deep dimples. Add flaky salt and properly baptised rosemary. Infant baptism or full immersion are both acceptable; the recipe takes no doctrinal position. For an extra-luscious surface, spoon the optional water-and-oil brine into the dimples.

Just before baking
8

Bake hot

Preheat a Smeg fan oven thoroughly to 220°C fan. Bake on the lower-middle rack until the top is deep golden and the underside is crisp. The centre should reach 97–99°C. If the top colours too fast, loosely cover with foil and finish the centre. Do not open the oven every four minutes to ask how it is going.

About 20–25 minutes
9

Get it out of the tray

Leave it only 3–5 minutes, then loosen the edges and lift it from the tray while still warm. This keeps the base crisp and reduces the chance of a wet focaccia welding itself to the pan and entering into a legally binding tenancy agreement. Rest briefly before cutting.

Eat warm

The ancient Italian secret*

It is deliberately wetter than ordinary focaccia, but the vital wheat gluten and folds give it enough structure to trap the gas. High hydration, restrained handling, a proper final proof and plenty of olive oil on a hot metal tray give you the open, almost custardy crumb and crisp fried-style base. *The ancient Italians were not consulted, and would probably have several concerns about the vital wheat gluten.
